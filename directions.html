Research: what real B2B marketplaces actually look like

Ten sites reviewed — Alibaba, OfBusiness, IndiaMART Export, Made-in-China, Thomasnet, McMaster-Carr, Grainger, Faire, Amazon Business, Tradeling. The pattern is consistent: these sites were built by engineering teams over 10–20 years, and they look it — small type, hard edges, one brand colour, and density that comes from real catalogue data, not from a design system template.

Known findings — verified against the ten sites

  • Body text 12–14px — confirmed. Every utilitarian site sits at 12–14px. 16px body text is a consumer/SaaS habit and instantly reads as a landing page, not a tool.
  • Results headings no larger than ~20–24px — confirmed. Inside the app, nothing is display-sized. Big type is reserved for marketing pages these companies keep separate from the catalogue.
  • One font family — confirmed, with one exception. Nine of ten use a single grotesque family in 2–3 weights. Faire is the exception: a licensed serif at regular weight for headings, which is exactly why it feels premium rather than editorial.
  • Corner radius 2–4px — confirmed on the catalogue sites (McMaster is near 0). Radius grows only on the marketing-led sites (Alibaba, Tradeling). 12–16px radius + pill buttons is the single strongest “template” tell.
  • 2–3 neutral greys + one brand colour — confirmed. Alibaba orange, Grainger red, Thomasnet blue. Nobody runs a palette; they run one colour and greys.
  • Buttons 32–42px tall — confirmed. Compact, rectangular, one primary action per row. No floating pill CTAs.
  • Dense list rows — confirmed. Rows carry photo, specs, supplier, trust signals and price tiers in one horizontal band. Buyers scan vertically and compare — grids break comparison.
  • The templated look has a fingerprint: Inter + slate greys, 12–16px radius on everything, soft shadows, pill buttons, gradient hero. That combination appears in no real marketplace above — it is what off-the-shelf website templates produce, which is why it reads as generic at a glance.

One business finding that shapes all three directions: none of the ten shows a firm delivered-to-door price for cross-border orders. The Asian marketplaces stop at FOB + “contact supplier”; the US catalogues are domestic only. Prime Cargo's one delivered price at checkout (sea or air, duty and trucking included, 30% now / 70% at shipment) is the differentiator — so in every direction below, browsing shows the factory price and the buy box is built around the delivered price.

Direction 1 — “Industrial catalog”

Borrows from Thomasnet, McMaster-Carr and Grainger. Dense, precise, engineered. White and grey, one strong blue, everything in its place. This is the direction that looks most like a tool a plant engineer already trusts — spec tables, tight rows, no decoration. Deliberately not a plain grey McMaster clone: the brand blue carries the header actions, and the delivered-price buy box — which McMaster doesn't have — is the visual centre of the product page.

Direction 2 — “Calm premium wholesale”

Borrows from Faire, applied to industrial goods. More white space, larger photography, headings in a serif at regular weight — never bold — and a single deep green used sparingly. The bet: a US buyer who has been burned by chaotic sourcing sites reads calm as competence. The risk is the opposite read: at $14,800 a set, some procurement managers may find it too quiet and go looking for the “real” catalogue.

Direction 3 — “Global trade, made simple”

Borrows from Alibaba and Made-in-China, cleaned up. Supplier-first: the maker's name, verification and track record sit directly under the product name, and the price is the boldest element on every row — because on a marketplace the buyer is comparing offers, not reading a catalogue. One confident orange accent. This keeps the structure Indian suppliers already know from Alibaba, minus the clutter, banners and badge spam.

Recommendation

Build Direction 3 — “Global trade, made simple” — for launch. The test is “does this look like a real marketplace that real sellers use?” Direction 3 is the only one whose structure a skeptical buyer — and a skeptical supplier — already believes, because Alibaba proved it at scale. It puts the two things that decide a cross-border order, price and supplier proof, in the row where the comparison happens, and it carries Prime Cargo's one real advantage — the delivered price — without changing a layout buyers have to learn. Direction 1 is the strongest second choice and the right skin for the industrial-equipment category pages later; Direction 2 is the best-looking and the riskiest, because its calm depends entirely on photography Prime Cargo doesn't have yet.

One more reason specific to this business: suppliers are often one person on a phone in Ahmedabad or Morbi. Direction 3's mobile results and product screens mirror the Alibaba app patterns they already use, so listing and replying will feel familiar on day one. Directions 1 and 2 both ask suppliers to learn a new visual language; Direction 3 only asks buyers to notice a cleaner one.

Five things that would make Direction 3 look even more real

  1. Real factory photography. Phone photos from each supplier's floor — products mid-assembly, the test bench, cartons being packed — slightly imperfect beats polished. Real photos are the single biggest gap between these mockups and a believable marketplace.
  2. Real supplier data with variance. Actual reply times, shipment counts and reorder rates per supplier, all slightly different. Uniform “98% on-time” on every row reads as invented; messy real numbers read as audited.
  3. Destination-specific prices. Delivered totals that genuinely change by ZIP code and week (freight moves). A buyer who checks Jersey City and Houston and sees the same number will assume the price is fake.
  4. Signs of other buyers. “Last ordered 6 days ago by a distributor in Ohio”, a short review naming a company and a use case, order counts per listing. Marketplaces look real because other people are visibly using them.
  5. A licensed typeface and wordmark later. Roboto is the honest free stand-in; licensing a grotesque with its own character (and drawing a real “Prime Cargo People” wordmark) is the finishing step that separates a brand from a well-made clone — worth doing once the direction is approved, not before.
